# CAP-ATLAS-PERCORSI — Research Reasoning: Emotion, Learning and Minor Protection

**Gate:** G1 — Discovery  
**Status:** RESEARCH_REASONING / NOT_A_SPECIFICATION  
**Related:** `research-evidence-register.md`, `pedagogical-model.md`, `emotional-development-safeguards.md`, `narrative-experience-bible.md`

## Why this record exists

This document preserves not only research findings but the reasoning that connects them to Atlas Percorsi. The purpose is to avoid reconstructing the same analysis in later phases, while keeping evidence, interpretation, uncertainty and governed decisions distinct.

## Starting question

Atlas Percorsi concerns minors' growth, education and learning and may involve emotions. How can an experiential and potentially narrative environment use emotion pedagogically without turning emotion into a mechanism for attention capture, psychological assessment, profiling, disclosure pressure or manipulation?

## Evidence-informed interpretation

### 1. Emotion is relevant to learning, but relevance is not licence to infer the learner

Research and institutional frameworks on social-emotional development support treating emotional processes as connected with learning, relationships, motivation and well-being. This supports designing experiences in which learners can recognise situations, perspectives, choices and consequences.

It does **not** support inferring a child's emotional state, personality or psychological condition from interaction data. Population-level associations cannot be converted into individual diagnosis or prediction.

**Atlas implication:** design for emotional literacy and reflective action, not emotional classification.

### 2. A memorable emotional image is not evidence of learning

Children can be strongly attracted to images and emotionally salient material. Salience may support orientation and memory, but it can also capture attention without producing conceptual integration or transfer. Therefore recall of an image, scene or character cannot by itself demonstrate competence development.

**Atlas implication:** after meaningful imagery, require an age-appropriate cognitive operation: recognise, choose, connect, order, explain, reconsider, retrieve or transfer. Do not respond to every image with another novel image merely to sustain attention.

### 3. Emotional intensity must not become the engagement strategy

A learning environment for minors must not optimise for repeated emotional arousal. Fear, shame, guilt, urgency, social comparison or attachment pressure may increase immediate engagement while damaging autonomy, safety or reflection.

**Atlas implication:** emotional significance should serve comprehension and reflection. The rhythm should permit orientation and pause:

`EMOTIONAL/MEANINGFUL STIMULUS → ORIENTATION → CHOICE/ACTION → PAUSE → REFLECTION/RECALL → TRANSFER`

not:

`STIMULUS → STRONGER STIMULUS → NOVEL STIMULUS → REWARD → REPEAT`.

### 4. Reflection must not require intimate disclosure

Activities can address emotions through fictional, hypothetical, third-person or situational material. A learner should not have to reveal family circumstances, health, trauma, relationships or other sensitive experiences to demonstrate learning.

**Atlas implication:** prefer safe distance when the pedagogical objective does not require autobiography. Personal reflection, where useful, should remain private/local by default and skippable without penalty.

### 5. Agency must be real but developmentally bounded

Choice can support autonomy, but too many choices or unclear consequences can increase cognitive burden. The appropriate degree of autonomy varies with age, context, prior knowledge and the activity itself.

**Atlas implication:** provide meaningful, understandable choices with visible consequences and recovery paths. Do not confuse autonomy with absence of structure.

### 6. The metaphor must never hide the educational action

Narrative and metaphor can create coherence and atmosphere, but younger learners and learners with language, attention or comprehension difficulties may interpret metaphors differently from adults.

**Atlas implication:** the learner must always be able to understand what to do without decoding the metaphor. Literal equivalents and low-stimulation variants are part of the design, not fallbacks added later.

### 7. Human mediation remains part of the pedagogical system

A public Atlas experience can support autonomous use, but some emotionally sensitive themes may require optional or required adult/teacher mediation. The classification belongs to the activity, not to a judgement about the child.

**Atlas implication:** every scene/pathway that touches emotionally significant material should declare one of:

- public autonomous use;
- optional adult/teacher mediation;
- adult/teacher mediation required.

The criterion and rationale must be documented.

## Consequence analysis

For emotionally meaningful design, the relevant consequences include:

- **attention:** does the stimulus orient or merely capture?;
- **memory:** is the learner integrating meaning or replacing one salient image with the next?;
- **emotion:** does the experience support recognition/regulation or exploit arousal?;
- **privacy:** can participation occur without disclosing personal experience?;
- **identity:** does feedback concern an action/strategy or label the learner?;
- **agency:** can the learner pause, skip, return or recover without penalty?;
- **accessibility:** is meaning available without animation, audio, metaphor or high visual density?;
- **transfer:** can the learner use the insight in a changed context?;
- **mediation:** is an adult role needed and, if so, is it explicit?;
- **sustainability:** are images, motion and generated assets pedagogically necessary rather than churn for attention?

## Design test derived from the reasoning

A future emotionally meaningful scene should not pass design review merely because it is attractive or memorable. It should answer:

1. What is the pedagogical objective?
2. Why is emotion relevant to that objective?
3. What is the dominant attentional focus?
4. Which visual/narrative elements are necessary and which are decorative?
5. What action demonstrates processing beyond image recall?
6. Can the learner complete the activity without personal disclosure?
7. What can be skipped or paused?
8. What feedback is given, and does it avoid identity/personality judgement?
9. What literal/low-stimulation/accessibility equivalent exists?
10. What transfer task follows?
11. What mediation level applies?
12. What foreseeable adverse consequence can be prevented or eliminated before implementation?

## What the research does NOT establish

The current evidence does not establish that:

- all children process images in the same way;
- emotional material necessarily improves learning;
- narrative immersion is superior to literal presentation;
- more interactivity produces more competence;
- a child's clickstream reveals emotion or competence;
- one metaphor will work equally across primary and lower-secondary age bands;
- remembering a scene demonstrates transfer;
- self-report is always reliable or always appropriate for minors.

These remain reasons for testing and cautious design, not gaps to fill with assumptions.

## Negative knowledge preserved

- Do not use emotion as an attention-retention mechanism.
- Do not infer emotional or psychological states from behavioural traces.
- Do not require sensitive autobiographical disclosure for ordinary competence activities.
- Do not equate visual recall with learning.
- Do not use novelty as the default response to declining attention.
- Do not make metaphor comprehension a prerequisite for task comprehension.
- Do not treat population research as a diagnostic model for an individual child.
