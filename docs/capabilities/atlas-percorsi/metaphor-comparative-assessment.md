# CAP-ATLAS-PERCORSI — Metaphor Comparative Assessment

**Gate:** G1 — Discovery  
**Status:** COMPARATIVE_ANALYSIS / NO_SELECTION  
**Depends on:** `pedagogical-model.md`, `curriculum-metaphor-matrix.md`, `narrative-experience-bible.md`, `safety-privacy.md`, `decision-rationale-sustainability.md`

## Purpose

Compare metaphor families before subject/script/UI work. This assessment deliberately avoids selecting a winner at G1. It records strengths, failure modes, age/accessibility implications and prototype obligations so that later human selection is evidence-bound rather than aesthetic-first.

## Evidence boundary

Current research supports caution rather than a universal metaphor prescription:

- metaphor comprehension develops with language, analogical reasoning, executive functions and prior knowledge;
- visual analogical relations differ in difficulty for children, with object/colour/quantity/size generally easier than orientation/position in one large school-age study;
- children's own representations of learning are multidimensional and do not simply reproduce metaphors proposed by adult learning theories;
- metaphors can scaffold thinking, but evidence linking a particular metaphor to learning behaviour/outcomes is context-dependent;
- therefore Atlas must never require successful decoding of an abstract metaphor in order to understand the learning task.

These findings inform design constraints; they do not establish that one metaphor family is pedagogically superior.

## Non-negotiable comparison criteria

Each candidate must be assessed against:

1. **pedagogical coherence** — supports awareness → choice → action → reflection → transfer;
2. **developmental robustness** — can be made concrete for younger learners and non-infantilising for older learners;
3. **literal fallback** — every action remains understandable without metaphor decoding;
4. **attention economy** — supports stable anchors rather than continuous visual replacement;
5. **cognitive economy** — does not require remembering arbitrary lore, symbols or navigation rules;
6. **agency** — offers meaningful bounded choices without choice overload;
7. **transfer** — can explicitly bridge experience to a different real context;
8. **accessibility/inclusion** — meaning is not dependent on sight, colour, motion, sound or spatial inference alone;
9. **privacy** — works without identity/profile/disclosure mechanics;
10. **non-comparative progress** — supports evidence/progression without leaderboards or identity scores;
11. **curriculum legibility** — teacher can trace experiences to governed competence/curriculum references;
12. **sustainability** — does not inherently demand heavy media, constant animation or third-party runtime services.

## Candidate A — Journey / crossroads

### Pedagogical affordance
A journey naturally represents sequence, choice, revisiting and transfer. Crossroads can represent bounded decisions without converting the learner into a score.

### Strengths
- intuitive action vocabulary: begin, choose, pause, return, continue;
- can preserve stable landmarks;
- naturally accommodates a learner-controlled notebook/map artefact;
- can make transfer visible as using a discovered tool in a new place;
- supports multiple pathways without requiring a linear course catalogue.

### Failure modes
- easily collapses into decorative maps, badges and level progression;
- spatial navigation may become cognitively demanding;
- can imply that competence is a destination that is permanently completed;
- fantasy/travel aesthetics may infantilise lower-secondary learners if over-authored;
- constant scene/location replacement risks the image-replacement treadmill.

### Prototype obligation
Test a minimal crossroads scene where the same stable landmark persists across choice, reflection and transfer. Provide an equivalent literal list/step representation.

## Candidate B — Laboratory / tools

### Pedagogical affordance
Competence can be framed as selecting, testing and refining strategies/tools for situations rather than possessing fixed traits.

### Strengths
- strong fit with action, experimentation, feedback and strategy selection;
- supports the principle that a tool is useful only in context;
- naturally avoids identity scoring: the learner acquires/revises strategies rather than labels;
- can remain visually economical through a stable workspace;
- transfer can be represented as selecting a familiar tool for a new problem.

### Failure modes
- may over-intellectualise emotional/social domains;
- 'toolbox' language can make complex relational competences appear mechanistic;
- laboratory imagery can drift toward school-subject/science ownership;
- accumulating many tools can create icon overload and memory burden.

### Prototype obligation
Test one stable workbench with no more than a small set of meaningful strategy objects and a non-visual/literal equivalent. Verify whether social/emotional experiences still feel humane rather than procedural.

## Candidate C — Observatory / lenses

### Pedagogical affordance
Different lenses can represent noticing self, others, context and consequences before acting.

### Strengths
- excellent fit with observation, perspective-taking and metacognition;
- can teach that changing perspective changes what becomes salient;
- stable observatory can reduce scene churn;
- lends itself to deliberate pauses rather than continuous stimulation.

### Failure modes
- weaker representation of action and transfer unless deliberately added;
- lens symbolism may be abstract for younger children;
- visual emphasis risks excluding learners if equivalents are weak;
- can privilege 'looking/thinking' over doing, collaborating and changing a situation.

### Prototype obligation
Test a literal 'notice two things, choose one action' scene in parallel with the lens metaphor. The learner must succeed without understanding the symbolic lens.

## Candidate D — Ecosystem / connections

### Pedagogical affordance
Represents interdependence, consequences, relationships and the fact that competences interact rather than exist as isolated modules.

### Strengths
- strong fit with responsibility, citizenship, sustainability and relational thinking;
- can show consequences and feedback loops;
- avoids simplistic linear progression;
- aligns with the broader TRAMA concern that actions have consequences.

### Failure modes
- conceptually dense for younger learners;
- risks visually crowded node/network interfaces;
- causal relations may be inferred incorrectly from decorative connections;
- difficult to represent private self-reflection without turning the learner into a node/profile;
- potentially expensive visually/technically if implemented as animated networks.

### Prototype obligation
Test a very small cause/consequence scene with 3–4 elements, not a network dashboard. Require verbal/literal explanation of every connection.

## Candidate E — Map / constellation of experiences

### Pedagogical affordance
Represents orientation and relationships among experiences while allowing multiple entry points.

### Strengths
- useful for overview and return/navigation;
- supports non-linear pathways;
- can expose connections among territories without declaring a fixed sequence;
- potentially strong as a teacher-facing overview.

### Failure modes
- spatial relations/orientation can impose avoidable cognitive load;
- constellation graphics can become decorative and semantically opaque;
- risks confusing navigation map with competence model;
- progress may accidentally become territory acquisition/completion;
- accessibility requires a fully equivalent structured representation.

### Prototype obligation
Treat the map as optional overview, never as the only navigation mechanism. Test equivalent hierarchical/list navigation and recovery after interruption.

## Comparative synthesis — G1

No candidate is rejected outright yet, but the evidence and failure analysis suggest a useful distinction:

- **Journey/crossroads** and **laboratory/tools** currently offer the clearest concrete action grammars for learner-facing prototypes;
- **observatory/lenses** appears promising as a secondary reflective device rather than necessarily the whole world;
- **ecosystem/connections** is conceptually valuable but carries higher density/abstraction risk for a primary learner-facing shell;
- **map/constellation** may be more robust as orientation/overview than as the sole learning metaphor.

This is a **prototype-priority hypothesis, not a winner ranking or design decision**.

## Hybrid warning

Do not combine all attractive metaphors into one world. A journey + toolbox + lenses + ecosystem + constellation can create symbolic overload. A later hybrid is permitted only if each metaphor has one stable, non-overlapping pedagogical function and usability evidence shows that learners understand the relationship.

## Age-band prototype matrix

Before selection, at least the two most promising learner-facing candidates must each be expressed as three concept scenes:

### Earlier primary
- concrete situation;
- one dominant visual focus;
- maximum small bounded choice set;
- short literal instruction;
- action before extended introspection;
- immediate reorientation affordance;
- no abstract progress label.

### Later primary
- explicit strategy naming;
- compare two plausible choices;
- visible action → consequence relation;
- short reflection and transfer;
- stable visual anchor retained.

### Lower-secondary
- authentic trade-off;
- less decorative framing;
- greater agency without loss of scaffolding;
- explicit transfer to another context;
- language and visual treatment that do not feel juvenile.

## Required accessibility variants for every concept scene

- literal/no-metaphor version;
- reduced-motion/no-auto-transition version;
- non-audio equivalent;
- non-colour-dependent cues;
- structured keyboard/assistive-technology-compatible order;
- low-stimulation presentation;
- recovery state after interruption;
- explanation of any symbol before it becomes necessary.

## Comprehension checks for later human testing

Testing must not ask only whether learners 'like' the scene. Observe whether they can:

1. state what they are being asked to do;
2. identify the meaningful choice;
3. explain the consequence without relying on decorative details;
4. recall the stable concept after a short interruption;
5. use the idea in a changed context;
6. navigate/recover without adult rescue where age-appropriate;
7. distinguish the metaphorical story from the real learning idea.

No personal profile is required to collect these observations during a governed, adult-mediated pilot.

## Negative knowledge added

### NK-011 — Metaphor collage
Do not combine multiple metaphor systems merely because each is individually attractive. Symbolic density can defeat comprehension.

### NK-012 — Preference equals learning
Do not select a metaphor because children report liking it. Preference is useful evidence about experience, not proof of comprehension, retention or transfer.

### NK-013 — Spatial beauty equals navigability
Do not assume a visually impressive map/network is an effective navigation structure, particularly for younger learners or users with spatial/cognitive accessibility needs.

### NK-014 — Adult metaphor projection
Do not assume metaphors created by designers/educators correspond to children's own representations of learning. Prototype and test comprehension.
