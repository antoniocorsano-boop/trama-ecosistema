# CAP-ATLAS-PERCORSI — Controlled Metaphor Comparison v1

**Gate:** G1 — Discovery  
**Status:** CONCEPT_PROTOTYPE / NOT_USER_VALIDATED  
**Purpose:** compare metaphor families while holding the pedagogical objective constant  
**Variants:** Literal · Journey/Crossroads · Workshop/Tools  
**Age bands:** early primary · later primary · lower secondary

## 1. Why this prototype exists

A metaphor must not be selected because it is attractive, memorable or easy to animate. This prototype holds the learning situation constant and varies only the narrative framing so that we can inspect what the metaphor adds, obscures or burdens.

This is a conceptual comparison, not evidence of effectiveness. No child-facing deployment or testing is authorised by this document.

## 2. Common pedagogical situation

### Situation: "The group has to choose how to continue"

A small group is carrying out a shared classroom task. Two possible ways forward emerge. One is quick and lets one person decide for everyone; the other takes slightly longer but gives each person a chance to contribute. A fictional peer appears uncertain and has not yet spoken.

The learner is not asked to disclose a personal conflict, friendship or emotional experience.

### Constant learning objective

Support the learner in:

- noticing that different people may have different perspectives;
- recognising that a choice can affect participation and relationships;
- selecting a strategy and giving an age-appropriate reason;
- reconsidering the choice after seeing a consequence;
- transferring the principle to a different, non-identical situation.

### What this does NOT assess

The scene does not assess whether the learner is "empathetic", "collaborative", "kind", "mature" or any other identity/personality trait. It provides evidence only about the action/reasoning shown in this bounded situation.

### Common safety constraints

- fictional third-person situation;
- no free-text autobiographical disclosure required;
- no microphone, camera, location or upload;
- no server-side learner profile;
- no emotional-state inference;
- no social ranking;
- pause/exit/restart available without penalty;
- personal reflection, if offered, remains optional and local;
- imagery cannot be required to decode the task.

## 3. Common cognitive sequence

All nine variants use the same sequence:

`ORIENT → NOTICE → CHOOSE → SEE CONSEQUENCE → RECONSIDER → TRANSFER`

No variant may add a reward loop or additional novelty merely to make the metaphor appear more engaging.

# 4. EARLY PRIMARY — conceptual prototype

## 4.1 Literal version

### Scene

Three fictional children are shown around a simple shared construction. Two large, stable choice cards appear:

A. "Decide now and continue."  
B. "Ask everyone for one idea, then choose."

One child is visually present but has not spoken. The learner chooses A or B.

### Consequence

A short static/low-motion update shows what happens next. If A is chosen, the group moves quickly but one idea is missing. If B is chosen, the group takes another step before deciding and gains an additional idea. Neither branch labels the learner as good/bad.

### Reflection action

The learner selects one of three reasons that best explains what changed. No personal disclosure.

### Transfer

A second situation changes the context from construction to choosing roles for a simple class activity. The learner identifies which action would help everyone take part.

### Attention budget

- one dominant illustration;
- maximum two primary choices at once;
- stable character positions across consequence states;
- no decorative animation;
- change highlighted only where pedagogically relevant.

### Mediation

Public autonomous use candidate; teacher mediation optional.

## 4.2 Journey/Crossroads version

### Metaphorical frame

The same group reaches a fork in a path while carrying a shared project marker. One route sign says "Go now"; the other says "Hear one idea each".

### What the metaphor adds

The fork makes the existence of a consequential choice concrete and visually spatial.

### What it risks

The learner may interpret one road as inherently "right" because of visual treatment. Therefore both paths must have neutral visual weight and the literal choice text must remain visible.

### Consequence

The path advances one step. The environment does not explode into a new scene; the same landmarks remain. A missing/added contribution is represented by the project itself, not by an emotional punishment.

### Transfer

A new crossroads appears in a different context, but the road imagery is secondary to literal instructions.

### Low-stimulation equivalent

Static fork diagram with text labels; no movement required.

## 4.3 Workshop/Tools version

### Metaphorical frame

The group has a small workbench. Two tools are available:

- "Quick choice";
- "One idea each".

The learner selects which tool to use for the shared task.

### What the metaphor adds

It represents strategies as tools that can be selected for a purpose rather than as fixed personal qualities.

### What it risks

Young children may focus on the physical attractiveness of the tool rather than its function. Tool appearance must therefore be simple and semantically neutral; literal labels remain primary.

### Consequence

The same workbench remains visible. The project changes minimally to show the consequence.

### Transfer

The learner sees a different task and chooses which strategy-tool fits the new situation.

### Low-stimulation equivalent

Two labelled strategy cards with small tool icons.

# 5. LATER PRIMARY — conceptual prototype

## 5.1 Literal version

### Scene

Four fictional classmates must decide how to organise a short group task. Two students favour a fast decision; another has not contributed. The learner chooses among three bounded strategies:

1. vote immediately;
2. hear one idea from each person, then decide;
3. let the person who proposed the task decide.

### Reasoning

After choosing, the learner selects the reason that best matches the intended strategy. Distractor reasons are plausible but not shaming.

### Consequence and reconsideration

The learner sees a concise consequence and may keep or revise the choice, with no penalty for revision. Revision is framed as evidence of thinking, not failure.

### Transfer

A different scenario concerns planning how to divide limited materials. The learner chooses what information should be gathered before deciding.

### Mediation

Public autonomous use candidate; optional classroom discussion can deepen transfer.

## 5.2 Journey/Crossroads version

### Frame

The shared task becomes a route with three possible crossings. Each route represents one strategy. Literal strategy text remains visible.

### Added value hypothesis

The route can externalise that decisions have consequences and can sometimes be revised before proceeding too far.

### Failure mode

A visually shorter route could imply that speed is preferred; a brighter route could imply correctness. Route length, colour salience and animation must not encode moral judgement accidentally.

### Reconsideration

After seeing a consequence, the learner can return to the junction. Returning is not represented as losing progress.

## 5.3 Workshop/Tools version

### Frame

Three strategy-tools are placed on a stable work surface: vote, round of ideas, proposer decides.

### Added value hypothesis

The tool model may support metacognition: a strategy is something selected according to the situation, not a trait possessed by a person.

### Failure mode

It may oversimplify social choices into mechanical operations. The scene therefore keeps the fictional peers and consequences visible; the tool is a scaffold, not the whole meaning.

### Reconsideration

The learner can put a tool back and choose another after observing its consequence.

# 6. LOWER SECONDARY — conceptual prototype

## 6.1 Literal version

### Scene

A fictional group has limited time to prepare a shared output. One member proposes deciding immediately; another argues that an unheard perspective could change the plan. The learner chooses among bounded strategies and receives enough contextual information to reason about time, inclusion and task quality.

### Reasoning

The learner selects or arranges reasons supporting the choice. A short optional local note may be offered, but it is not required, uploaded or used for scoring.

### Consequence

The result shows a trade-off rather than a simplistic moral answer. For example, consultation may improve the plan but consume time; immediate decision may preserve time but omit useful information.

### Reconsideration

The learner can revise the strategy after receiving one new piece of information.

### Transfer

A different scenario involves deciding how to respond when group members disagree about the reliability of a source. The learner identifies which decision process is appropriate and why.

### Mediation

Autonomous use candidate for ordinary scenarios; classroom mediation useful for discussing trade-offs and transfer.

## 6.2 Journey/Crossroads version

### Frame

The learner sees a restrained route map with decision points and limited resources such as time. The map represents consequences and reversibility rather than adventure/reward.

### Added value hypothesis

Can make trade-offs and path dependency visible.

### Developmental risk

May feel childish if rendered as a fantasy quest. For this age band the visual language must become diagrammatic, restrained and closer to an orientation map than a game world.

### Failure criterion

If the metaphor requires decorative world-building to remain appealing, or if students understand the literal version faster with equal transfer, the journey frame should be reduced or rejected for this band.

## 6.3 Workshop/Tools version

### Frame

Strategies become a compact decision toolkit: rapid decision, structured turn-taking, vote, evidence check, role rotation. Only tools relevant to the scene are shown.

### Added value hypothesis

Strong fit with strategy selection, metacognition and transfer: the learner can ask "which tool fits this situation and why?"

### Developmental risk

Can become an abstract dashboard resembling productivity software. The human situation and consequences must remain primary.

### Failure criterion

If learners focus on collecting/unlocking tools rather than reasoning about use, the metaphor has become a reward system and fails.

# 7. Controlled comparison matrix

| Criterion | Literal | Journey/Crossroads | Workshop/Tools |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Task clarity | baseline to beat | must remain equal to baseline | must remain equal to baseline |
| Consequence visibility | direct | potentially strong spatial representation | strong strategy→effect representation |
| Risk of visual capture | lowest candidate | medium if scenery grows | medium if tools become collectible/decorative |
| Risk of moral coding | wording-dependent | high if path styling implies right/wrong | medium if tool styling implies better/worse |
| Strategy transfer | explicit | must escape route metaphor | potentially strong if tools remain contextual |
| Age scalability | high | requires substantial tonal change | requires abstraction increase with age |
| Low-stimulation equivalent | native | straightforward | straightforward |
| Personal-data need | none | none | none |
| Emotional disclosure need | none | none | none |
| Primary failure signal | too dry/low coherence | metaphor dominates task | strategy becomes gamified object collection |

No winner is declared at G1 conceptual stage.

# 8. What must be observed before selection

A later governed validation should examine, using age-appropriate and privacy-preserving methods:

- can the learner state what the task asks without explaining the metaphor?;
- does the metaphor improve or reduce orientation?;
- what visual element is recalled, and is the pedagogical relation also recalled?;
- can the learner explain why a strategy fits?;
- can the learner transfer the principle to the changed context?;
- does the learner mistake visual salience for correctness?;
- does revision feel permitted or like failure?;
- can the learner pause and resume without losing orientation?;
- does a low-stimulation/literal version preserve the same educational meaning?;
- do any prompts trigger unnecessary personal disclosure?;
- does the experience remain age-respectful, particularly in lower secondary?

These questions must not become covert psychological profiling or individual behavioural scoring.

# 9. Prototype consequence review

## Foreseeable positive consequences

- clearer distinction between action and identity;
- explicit practice in considering perspectives and consequences;
- opportunity to revise decisions without punishment;
- transfer beyond the first scene;
- possible metacognitive support through stable visual scaffolds.

## Foreseeable adverse consequences

- visually attractive metaphor may dominate reasoning;
- path/tool styling may encode an unintended correct answer;
- repeated novelty may replace reflection;
- younger learners may interpret symbols literally;
- older learners may reject an infantilising atmosphere;
- social-emotional content may be mistaken for assessment of the learner;
- optional reflection could drift into personal disclosure if prompts are poorly written.

## Prevention

- literal task statement always available;
- neutral visual treatment of choices;
- stable scene anchors;
- no automatic image carousel or decorative motion;
- bounded fictional scenarios;
- feedback about choices/consequences, never personality;
- no server-side personal reflection;
- age-band-specific language and visual tone;
- explicit transfer task;
- low-stimulation equivalent designed from the start.

# 10. Decision state

**G1 result:** all three framings remain candidates. The conceptual comparison gives a stronger reason to prototype **Journey/Crossroads** and **Workshop/Tools** against a literal baseline, but does not establish superiority.

The next legitimate step is to turn these conceptual scenes into a **script/storyboard specification** with controlled visual variables and review it for pedagogy, child safety, accessibility, cognitive load and privacy before any polished UI or animation is built.
