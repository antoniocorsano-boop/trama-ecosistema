# Atlas Percorsi — Cognitive Load, Attention and Multimedia Screening v1

**Gate:** G1 — Discovery / industrialisation  
**Status:** ACTIVE SCREENING  
**Runtime:** NOT_AUTHORIZED  
**Date:** 2026-09-25

## Research question

How should cognitive-load theory, working-memory constraints, attention research and multimedia-learning evidence constrain the visual, narrative and interactive design of Atlas Percorsi for children and lower-secondary students?

## Method note

Discovery searches contained substantial irrelevant material. Search-result presence is not evidence adoption. This screening privileges mature instructional-design traditions, reviews/meta-analyses, and findings that can be translated conservatively to school-age interfaces. Age-specific evidence must remain separately tagged because much multimedia research uses adults or university students.

## Core position

Atlas Percorsi must not optimise for visual stimulation. It must optimise for **meaningful cognitive activity with controlled attentional demand**.

Images, characters, animation, sound, narrative and interaction are justified only when they perform a learning, orientation, motivational-without-distraction, accessibility or meaning-making function that can be stated explicitly.

## Evidence-informed directions

### 1. Working memory is constrained — SUPPORTED direction

Novel information and interacting elements can overload limited processing capacity, particularly when learners lack established schemas.

**Design consequence:** complexity must be staged. A child should not have to decode the interface, remember several rules, follow a narrative thread and solve the target competence problem simultaneously unless those demands are intentionally scaffolded.

### 2. Guidance depends on learner knowledge — SUPPORTED / CONTEXT-DEPENDENT

Instructional support useful to novices may become redundant for more knowledgeable learners. Conversely, minimally guided discovery can impose unnecessary search demands when prerequisite knowledge is weak.

**Design consequence:** Percorsi should provide progressive scaffolding and removable/reducible supports rather than assuming either permanent guidance or pure discovery.

### 3. Interesting but irrelevant details can impair learning — SUPPORTED direction

The seductive-details literature indicates that attractive but instructionally irrelevant additions can divert attention, disrupt coherence or encourage an inappropriate organising structure. Effects vary by context and design.

**Design consequence:** visual attractiveness is not sufficient justification. Every salient decorative element must survive a relevance test: does it support orientation, meaning, emotion regulation, accessibility, narrative causality or the competence task? If not, it is a candidate for removal.

### 4. Coherence and signalling matter — SUPPORTED direction

Learners benefit when design makes relevant relations easier to identify and avoids unnecessary competing material.

**Design consequence:** hierarchy, spatial grouping, progressive disclosure, cueing and restrained emphasis should direct attention toward the current decision or relation. The interface must not make every element equally salient.

### 5. Multimedia is not automatically superior — SUPPORTED methodological rule

Combining words, pictures, sound and animation can support learning when representations are complementary and cognitively integrated; redundant or poorly coordinated media can add processing demands.

**Design consequence:** Percorsi must select modality by function. Do not add narration, captions, animation and decorative imagery simultaneously merely because the platform can render them.

### 6. Active cognitive processing matters more than visible activity — SUPPORTED direction

Interaction is educationally useful when it prompts selecting, organising, explaining, comparing, generating, deciding or integrating relevant information. Clicking and moving through screens are not themselves evidence of meaningful processing.

**Design consequence:** interaction density is not a quality metric. Prefer fewer consequential interactions over continuous low-value taps.

## Special rule for children and visual memory

The project must explicitly guard against a **successive-image replacement pattern**: rapidly presenting memorable images that compete for attention without helping the learner build a stable mental model.

This is currently a **design-risk hypothesis requiring age-specific validation**, not a claim that children universally process images in one way.

Operational precautions:

- preserve stable visual anchors across related steps;
- avoid unnecessary scene replacement;
- use recurrence when an image represents a concept or relation;
- distinguish structural images from atmospheric images;
- allow enough time for inspection and reasoning;
- avoid reward animations that interrupt cognitive continuity;
- do not use novelty as the primary mechanism for sustaining engagement.

## Functional visual taxonomy

Every salient visual asset should be classified before production:

1. **STRUCTURAL** — represents a relation, process, choice space or concept;
2. **ORIENTING** — indicates place, sequence, status or next action;
3. **NARRATIVE-CAUSAL** — communicates a consequence or story relation necessary to the task;
4. **AFFECTIVE-SAFE** — supports tone, reassurance or emotional distance without manipulating the learner;
5. **ACCESSIBILITY** — improves comprehension or provides an alternative representation;
6. **ATMOSPHERIC** — contributes to world/identity but is not necessary for reasoning;
7. **DECORATIVE** — no demonstrated functional contribution.

`DECORATIVE` assets require explicit justification and are the first candidates for removal under cognitive-load review. `ATMOSPHERIC` assets require a bounded attention budget.

## Narrative consequence

Narrative should organise meaning, not compete with the learning problem. Characters are justified when they externalise viewpoints, consequences, social information, uncertainty or dialogue needed by the competence. A mascot whose main function is persistent stimulation is not automatically justified.

Manga/comic grammar, animation, simulation, theatre/viewpoint and other forms must therefore be selected pathway-by-pathway according to cognitive function. No style receives privileged status merely because it is attractive to young users.

## Interaction consequence

A candidate screen/scene budget should be reviewed across four demand classes:

- **task demand** — inherent complexity of the competence problem;
- **representation demand** — effort needed to understand visual/verbal representation;
- **navigation demand** — effort needed to operate the interface;
- **narrative demand** — effort needed to retain characters, events and causal context.

The product should minimise avoidable representation/navigation demand so cognitive resources can be allocated to task-relevant and meaning-relevant processing.

No universal numeric budget is authorised yet.

## Transfer connection

Transfer requires controlled variation. Multimedia variation should therefore change only what the transfer design intends to change. If visual/narrative novelty introduces unrelated differences, failure may measure interface adaptation rather than competence transfer.

For each changed-context task, record which visual/narrative dimensions changed and why.

## Child-safety and emotional boundary

Attention capture must never become coercive engagement. Percorsi must avoid dark patterns, artificial urgency, infinite loops, loss-framed streaks, manipulative scarcity, or emotional pressure to continue.

Characters and narrative must not pressure minors to disclose personal information or use simulated intimacy as a mechanism for retention. Reflection remains fictional, hypothetical or task-bound where possible.

## Candidate review questions for every scene

1. What is the learner meant to notice, think about or decide here?
2. Which elements are necessary for that process?
3. Which elements compete for attention?
4. Does the learner need to remember information from the previous scene? If so, is it externally supported?
5. Is an image carrying meaning or merely replacing the previous image?
6. Could the same learning function be achieved with less representational complexity?
7. Is the interaction cognitively consequential or merely navigational?
8. Is the emotional tone supportive without becoming manipulative?
9. Does the scene remain understandable without collecting personal data?
10. What evidence would show that the design helped rather than distracted?

## Claims explicitly not authorised

Current screening does not authorise:

- fixed universal limits on text, images, animation duration or number of interactions;
- the claim that children are intrinsically visual learners;
- learning-styles matching;
- the claim that more immersive media necessarily improve learning;
- the claim that animation is superior to static graphics in general;
- automatic use of gamification to maintain attention;
- a universal mascot or character system;
- treating engagement time as evidence of learning or competence.

## Evidence records required next

Normalisation should create evidence records for:

- cognitive load theory and instructional design;
- working memory and prior knowledge;
- coherence/seductive-details effects;
- signalling, contiguity, redundancy and modality;
- generative/active learning with multimedia;
- age/development differences in multimedia attention;
- animation versus static representation by task type;
- emotional design and learning;
- attention capture/digital engagement risks for minors;
- accessibility and reduced-motion implications.

Each record must state population, task, medium, comparison, learning outcome, transfer outcome where present, limitations and permitted design inference.

## Current decision

**Promote functional relevance, coherence, stable cognitive orientation and controlled attentional demand as G1 design constraints.**

Do not promote a fixed visual style, media quantity, interaction quota or universal cognitive-load threshold.

## Next dependency

The next synthesis should examine developmental evidence more directly: executive functions, metacognition/self-regulation, emotion and age-appropriate reflection in primary and lower-secondary learners. This will connect the cognitive architecture to the project's transversal-competence and child-safety constitution.
