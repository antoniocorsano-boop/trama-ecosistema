# Atlas Percorsi — Game-World Learning Experience Benchmark v1

**Status:** RESEARCH_SCREENING / PRODUCT_DIRECTION_INPUT / NOT_RUNTIME_AUTHORIZATION  
**Target:** first-cycle learner-facing Percorsi  
**Date:** 2026-10-04

## 1. Why this benchmark exists

The current PW-STRATEGY-SELECTION prototypes improved learner language, visual context and narrative presence but still failed Human Experience Review because they remained recognisable as **school exercises with a story layered on top**.

The benchmark question is therefore not:

> How can we make a worksheet-like pathway prettier or more narrative?

It is:

> What makes an educational digital experience meaningful enough that a lower-secondary learner has a reason to enter, stay, act, discover and return?

## 2. Academic evidence

### 2.1 Digital games: design matters more than medium

Clark, Tanner-Smith & Killingsworth (2016), *Digital Games, Design, and Learning*, Review of Educational Research.

- Meta-analysis across K–16.
- Digital games outperformed non-game conditions on learning on average.
- Value-added game designs also outperformed standard game designs.
- Effects varied with mechanics, visual and narrative characteristics.

Design implication:

**“Game” is not a sufficient product category.** The learning value comes from how mechanics, narrative, representation and feedback are designed together.

Source:
https://consensus.app/papers/digital-games-design-and-learning-clark-tanner%E2%80%90smith/7f61ea2b49bd5f60a86566d3a9390f68/?utm_source=chatgpt

### 2.2 Narrative game design

Naul & Liu (2019), *Why Story Matters: A Review of Narrative in Serious Games*, Journal of Educational Computing Research.

The review identifies four recurring narrative characteristics associated with effective serious-game experiences:

1. **distributed narrative**;
2. **intrinsically integrated fantasy/story world**;
3. **empathetic characters/agents**;
4. **adaptiveness/responsivity**.

Design implication:

A story should not be delivered in one explanatory block. It should be **distributed through the world, characters, objects, events and consequences**, and the experience should respond to the learner.

Source:
https://consensus.app/papers/why-story-matters-a-review-of-narrative-in-serious-games-naul-liu/5a439b4fc7c75823aa1636ed42c60435/?utm_source=chatgpt

### 2.3 Role-play and critical thinking

Mao et al. (2021), *Effects of Game-Based Learning on Students’ Critical Thinking: A Meta-Analysis*, Journal of Educational Computing Research.

- 20 empirical studies / 1,947 participants.
- Positive overall effect on critical thinking.
- Role-playing game formats showed the largest mean effect among the game types represented, although heterogeneity is substantial.

Design implication:

For complex reasoning, the learner may benefit from being **someone inside a problem**, not an outside respondent answering about the problem.

Source:
https://consensus.app/papers/effects-of-gamebased-learning-on-students%E2%80%99-critical-mao-cui/0cf1a487e0d55e8f8bf370977a139c30/?utm_source=chatgpt

### 2.4 Narrative role-play with 11–13 year olds

Huynh et al. (2020), *Designing Narrative-Focused Role-Playing Games for Visualization Literacy in Young Children*, IEEE TVCG.

- Participants: children aged 11–13.
- Narrative version took longer to complete.
- Narrative elements increased engagement and enjoyment without reducing learning.

Design implication:

Efficiency is not the only criterion. A richer narrative experience may legitimately take longer when it produces **greater engagement without sacrificing learning**.

Source:
https://consensus.app/papers/designing-narrativefocused-roleplaying-games-for-huynh-nyhout/8d03c663b97f5a0fa93836f392c5b4d7/?utm_source=chatgpt

### 2.5 Agency

Joshi et al. (2024), *Exploring player agency in educational video games*, Entertainment Computing.

The study focuses on learner autonomy over learning mechanics and reports design implications suggesting that player agency can increase enjoyment and intrinsic motivation without sacrificing educational effectiveness.

Design implication:

The learner should not merely choose the next correct card. They need **meaningful control over how to investigate, construct, test or proceed**.

Source:
https://consensus.app/papers/exploring-player-agency-in-educational-video-games-joshi-mousas/541d2df12bee5377814dcfcbbadec7f5/?utm_source=chatgpt

## 3. Mature educational precedents

These products/projects are not treated as interchangeable or as direct implementation templates. They are analysed for recurring experience structures.

---

## 3.1 Quest Atlantis

### What it was

Quest Atlantis was designed for children roughly 9–12, combining a multi-user virtual environment, educational quests, virtual personae, narrative and social commitments.

Barab et al. describe the design as sitting at the intersection of **education, entertainment and social commitment**.

Students:
- travelled through virtual spaces;
- performed quests;
- interacted with other users and mentors;
- built virtual personae;
- acted in problems embedded in a wider world.

Research:
https://consensus.app/papers/making-learning-fun-quest-atlantis-a-game-without-guns-barab-thomas/b6af6c28719e5657a84b933908c7c2fc/?utm_source=chatgpt

A later assessment study found that a 15-hour Quest Atlantis curriculum produced larger learning gains than an expository-text comparison and that engagement with formative feedback was associated with larger gains.

Research:
https://consensus.app/papers/designing-assessments-and-assessing-designs-in-virtual-hickey-ingram-goble/1712eec7bd2b51e4ad2fbfe273756b15/?utm_source=chatgpt

### What Atlas should learn

- There is a **world before the task**.
- Quests make sense because the world contains goals and problems.
- Identity/role is part of participation.
- Learning is linked to transforming or understanding the context.
- Feedback belongs to the experience, not to a detached quiz layer.

### What Atlas should not copy

- Multi-user identity/account requirements.
- Open social interaction between unknown students.
- Large persistent 3-D world architecture.

## 3.2 EcoMUVE

### What it is

EcoMUVE, developed at Harvard Graduate School of Education, is a middle-school ecosystems curriculum using immersive virtual ecosystems.

Students:
- explore authentic-looking environments;
- gather observations/data;
- work collaboratively;
- construct hypotheses;
- investigate causal dynamics.

Harvard description:
https://ecolearn.gse.harvard.edu/projects/ecomuve

Research describes teams of students constructing hypotheses from evidence collected through virtual experience; the learning process is situated inside the virtual ecosystem.

Research:
https://consensus.app/papers/collaborative-hypothesisbuilding-using-immersive-metcalf-kamarainen/8a0e7e6e6e4a509a899d595576179c52/?utm_source=chatgpt

### What Atlas should learn

The important feature is not 3-D.

The important feature is that the learner:
- **goes somewhere**;
- observes;
- collects information;
- sees a system change;
- builds an explanation from what happened inside the world.

The environment itself is an evidence source.

## 3.3 River City

### What it was

River City was a Harvard-developed interactive simulation for middle-grade science.

Students entered a 19th-century town experiencing health problems. They worked in research teams to understand why residents were becoming ill.

They:
- explored the town;
- gathered clues;
- formed hypotheses;
- designed/tested investigations;
- made recommendations from evidence.

Harvard archive:
https://muve.gse.harvard.edu/

Research:
https://consensus.app/papers/a-multiuser-virtual-environment-for-building-higher-order-ketelhut-dede/63e1bb408f5e5e42bcaf58ea00e51f97/?utm_source=chatgpt

### What Atlas should learn

This is much closer to the desired Percorsi grammar than “read → sort → check”.

The learner has:
- a **place**;
- a **problem affecting that place**;
- incomplete evidence;
- agency to investigate;
- hypotheses;
- consequences/recommendations.

The curricular knowledge becomes useful because the world demands it.

## 3.4 Minecraft Education

### What it provides

Minecraft Education is an immersive game-based platform used by schools internationally.

Its educational model combines:
- open/creative worlds;
- building;
- exploration;
- multiplayer collaboration;
- challenges;
- curriculum-aligned content.

Official:
https://education.minecraft.net/

Minecraft Education reports use across more than 40,000 school systems in 140 countries and offers hundreds of curriculum resources and immersive worlds.

A 2025 systematic review of 29 intervention studies found promising results in areas including spatial thinking, creativity, critical thinking, mathematics, science and language, while also finding medium/high risk of bias across the evidence base.

Research:
https://consensus.app/papers/assessing-the-benefits-of-digital-game%E2%80%90based-learning-slattery-lehane/14013d7f6e765b47854e31331e5216b1/?utm_source=chatgpt

### What Atlas should learn

Minecraft does not keep students engaged because an instructional card tells them to click.

It gives them:
- space;
- materials;
- freedom;
- construction;
- visible consequences;
- ownership of the result.

### What Atlas should not copy

Atlas does not need:
- voxel graphics;
- full sandbox complexity;
- multiplayer accounts;
- endless worlds.

The relevant principle is **productive agency**.

## 3.5 Mission US

### What it is

Mission US is designed for middle-school learners and uses interactive narrative games around historical problems.

The project explicitly describes its model as:
- immersion in rich historical settings;
- role/perspective;
- choices;
- perspective-taking;
- weighing multiple kinds of evidence;
- no score/grade/win-loss requirement.

Official:
https://www.mission-us.org/
https://www.mission-us.org/about/

### What Atlas should learn

A learning experience can be compelling without points, streaks or leaderboards.

The source of engagement can instead be:
- **who am I here?**
- **what is happening?**
- **what should I do?**
- **whose perspective do I trust?**
- **what changes if I choose this?**

This strongly matches Atlas privacy-first and non-ranking requirements.

## 4. Cross-case synthesis

Across the strongest precedents, the recurring pattern is not:

`STORY SETUP → EDUCATIONAL EXERCISE → FEEDBACK → MORAL`

It is closer to:

`WORLD → ROLE → DISTURBANCE/PROBLEM → EXPLORE → DISCOVER → CHOOSE/BUILD/TEST → WORLD REACTS → REINTERPRET → ACT AGAIN → RESOLUTION/OPEN CONSEQUENCE`

### 4.1 The learner needs a reason to be there

The learner should not be asked to help merely because the interface says “help them”.

Their role must create **functional agency**.

Examples:
- field investigator;
- planner;
- junior researcher;
- designer;
- reporter;
- curator;
- technician;
- mediator;
- expedition member.

The role must change what information they can access or what they can do.

### 4.2 The world should contain information

Do not place all information in cards below the world.

Information may live in:
- places;
- objects;
- dialogue;
- environmental change;
- maps;
- instruments;
- character viewpoints;
- logs/messages;
- visual traces.

The learner should **inspect the world**, not merely read about it.

### 4.3 Meaningful agency is not “choose A/B/C”

Agency means the learner can decide:
- where to inspect;
- what evidence to collect;
- which representation to build;
- what hypothesis to test;
- which constraint to prioritise;
- which tool to use;
- what to construct;
- when to revise.

### 4.4 Consequences must be visible

The world should respond:
- route changes;
- character changes plan;
- model behaves differently;
- hypothesis becomes more/less plausible;
- design fails a constraint;
- environment changes;
- new information becomes available.

### 4.5 Learning should be embedded in productive action

The learner should use knowledge **because the mission requires it**, not because the system asks a curriculum question.

### 4.6 Closure should belong to the world

Avoid:
> “You have learned that…”

Prefer:
- the repaired system works;
- the team can finally decide;
- the report is accepted;
- a character changes their interpretation;
- the map/world is now understood;
- a new unresolved question opens the next pathway.

## 5. Product conclusion

The current Atlas Percorsi prototypes remain too close to interactive worksheets.

The next-generation model should be treated as a **Compact Learning World**, not a card flow.

### Proposed canonical grammar

`ENTER WORLD → RECEIVE ROLE → ENCOUNTER PROBLEM → EXPLORE → GATHER/CREATE → FORM INTERPRETATION → ACT → SEE CONSEQUENCE → REVISE → RESOLVE/OPEN NEXT QUESTION`

This can be implemented in lightweight 2-D/mobile form.

**World does not mean 3-D.**

A convincing world can be:
- an illustrated spatial scene;
- a map with inspectable locations;
- a workshop with active objects;
- a newsroom;
- a museum;
- a lab;
- a town;
- a building/site;
- a control room;
- a field trip;
- a design studio.

What matters is that the environment carries state, information and consequence.

## 6. Implication for PW-STRATEGY-SELECTION-01

Do not iterate Missione Belvedere as another sequence-card experience.

The cognitive target should be rebuilt inside a world in which **different information-organisation strategies become useful tools chosen by the learner to solve changing problems**.

A stronger candidate could allow the learner to:
- enter a field-team workspace;
- inspect the route/map/logs in any order;
- receive partial information from characters;
- construct a timeline when chronology matters;
- later discover that the timeline cannot answer the new causal question;
- switch to a causal map because the world now demands an explanation;
- see the team use the explanation to make a real next decision.

The educational principle is then **experienced as tool utility**, not stated as a lesson.

## 7. Product-development rule

Before implementation, every new Percorso concept must answer:

1. Why would a learner enter this world?
2. What role do they have?
3. What can they do that matters?
4. Where does information live?
5. What can they discover rather than be told?
6. What changes because of their action?
7. What are they constructing/producing?
8. Why does the learning target become useful inside the world?
9. What would make them curious enough to continue?
10. Would the experience still be worthwhile if all badges and progress indicators disappeared?

If these answers are weak, do not prototype the interface yet.
