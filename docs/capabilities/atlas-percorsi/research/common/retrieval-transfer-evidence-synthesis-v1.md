# Atlas Percorsi — Retrieval Practice & Transfer: Evidence Synthesis v1

## Research question

What can mature research on retrieval practice, spacing and transfer legitimately contribute to the design of Atlas Percorsi for primary and lower-secondary learners, without confusing memory performance with transversal competence?

## Method note

Broad OpenAlex searches produced substantial domain noise (including machine-learning uses of “transfer”). The search protocol was therefore tightened using education/cognitive-science phrases, including `retrieval practice`, `transfer`, `spacing`, `education`, `classroom`, and age/context-specific follow-up terms.

Candidate sources are screened for population, learning task, outcome and whether they measure retention, near transfer or far transfer. Search appearance alone is not evidence adoption.

## Candidate mature anchors identified

The screening surfaced several relevant evidence families, including:

- reviews/syntheses on retrieval-based learning at the cognitive-science/education interface;
- recent synthesis work on spacing and retrieval practice;
- work specifically examining specificity/enrichment and transfer conditions;
- classroom applications of spaced retrieval;
- primary-school work comparing retrieval practice with other instructional supports for mathematical problem solving;
- secondary-education applications, including vocabulary learning.

These sources require individual evidence records before any strong pathway-specific claim is promoted.

## What is sufficiently established to guide research architecture

### 1. Retrieval practice is a learning mechanism, not a competence measure — SUPPORTED

Repeated effortful retrieval can strengthen later access to learned knowledge relative to passive restudy under many conditions. This justifies studying retrieval as one possible component of a learning sequence.

It does **not** justify treating a correct recall, quiz score, repeated answer or completed game as evidence that a transversal competence has been acquired.

### 2. Retention and transfer must be separated — SUPPORTED

A learner remembering previously studied material is not equivalent to applying a strategy in a meaningfully changed situation. Atlas evidence records and validation plans must therefore distinguish:

- retention/reproduction;
- near transfer: changed example/task with substantial structural similarity;
- farther transfer: changed context, surface features and/or task demands requiring recognition and adaptation of the underlying strategy.

### 3. Retrieval success depends on instructional conditions — SUPPORTED AS A GENERAL CAUTION

The literature indicates that effects are moderated by conditions such as successful retrieval, feedback, spacing, question/task form, prior knowledge and difficulty. Therefore “add a quiz” is not an evidence-based Atlas design rule.

### 4. Transfer is not guaranteed by retrieval alone — SUPPORTED

Evidence for remembering learned material is broader and more stable than evidence for far transfer. Transfer depends on what is retrieved, how knowledge is represented, similarity/variation across tasks, prior knowledge, cues and opportunities to recognise when a strategy applies.

For Atlas, the important design question is therefore not “did the learner retrieve the answer?” but “can the learner recognise and adapt the relevant strategy when the situation changes?”

## Implications for Atlas Percorsi

### SUPPORTED / safe to make architectural

1. **Do not equate completion with competence.**
2. **Do not equate recall with competence.**
3. **Make transfer an explicit validation target rather than an assumed consequence of practice.**
4. **Record the distance of transfer being claimed.**
5. **Use changed contexts when evaluating whether a strategy can be recognised and adapted.**
6. **Separate learning support from assessment evidence.** A retrieval prompt may support learning without being an adequate competence assessment.
7. **Preserve learner feedback and opportunities to correct/revise rather than rewarding mere response production.**

### PLAUSIBLE / requires age- and pathway-specific validation

1. Spaced re-encounters with a previously recognised strategy may be preferable to massed repetition.
2. Retrieval of the *reasoning/strategy* rather than a verbal label may support later use more effectively for some transversal targets.
3. Variation in surface context may help prevent over-specific learning, provided cognitive load remains manageable.
4. Asking the learner to recognise when a strategy is useful may be more relevant to transfer than repeatedly naming the strategy.
5. Feedback after an unsuccessful attempt may be essential when the learner lacks sufficient prior knowledge.

These are design hypotheses, not current product requirements.

### NOT DEMONSTRATED / prohibited as claims

- “Quizzes build transversal competence.”
- “Retrieval practice guarantees far transfer.”
- “A learner who succeeds repeatedly in one narrative world has mastered the competence.”
- “More repetition is always better.”
- “Gamified recall is superior because engagement is higher.”
- “A score can infer a stable personal trait, emotional state or competence identity.”

## Consequence for pathway architecture

A competence pathway should be capable of representing a progression such as:

`SUPPORTED PRACTICE → RE-ENCOUNTER → CHANGED SURFACE CONTEXT → STRATEGY RECOGNITION → ADAPTATION → TRANSFER TASK → REFLECTION/TRACE`

This is a **research-informed candidate architecture**, not a mandatory fixed sequence. Each competence territory must establish whether this progression is appropriate.

## Consequence for game design

Game-like interaction can provide practice conditions, but game success must not become the competence model. Points, speed, streaks and repeated correct answers are especially poor proxies for transversal competence unless independently validated for a specific construct; they may also distort attention toward performance rather than strategy.

Atlas should preferentially observe task-relevant actions such as comparing information, revising a choice, identifying missing evidence, adapting a strategy or explaining a relation — while respecting the privacy/no-profiling constitution.

## Age/context boundary

A substantial portion of cognitive-science evidence is not specific to Italian primary/lower-secondary populations. Before adopting detailed parameters such as spacing intervals, task difficulty or feedback timing, Atlas must screen age-specific classroom evidence.

No universal spacing interval, number of repetitions, difficulty level or retrieval format is authorized by this synthesis.

## Next evidence work

1. Create individual evidence records for the strongest reviews/syntheses.
2. Screen primary and lower-secondary classroom studies separately.
3. Build a dedicated packet on **transfer of learning**, beyond retrieval practice.
4. Cross-link with cognitive-load/multimedia evidence before recommending context variation.
5. Examine metacognition and self-regulated learning for strategy recognition and learner-controlled reflection.
6. Only then update pathway/grammar requirements.

## Current conclusion

Retrieval practice is relevant to Atlas primarily as a possible **learning mechanism**. The central construct for Percorsi is broader: repeated opportunities to recognise, adapt and transfer strategies across changing contexts. The repository must therefore preserve evidence about transfer rather than allowing recall performance, engagement or game completion to stand in for competence.
